Before You React, Give Your Brain a Chance to Catch Up

Johnsi Jayasingh·August 23, 2026·4 min read

Your manager sends you a message: "Can we talk? I'm disappointed with how you handled this project."

Your heart starts racing. The thoughts move fast: Seriously? After everything I've done? They always blame me. Fine, maybe I should just resign.

Before you know it, you've typed: "If you think I'm not doing a good job, maybe you should find someone else."

Send.

Five minutes later, you regret it.

What just happened in your brain?

Your amygdala — the part of your brain that detects threats — can trigger a fight-or-flight response the moment something feels threatening, even if the "threat" is just an unsettling Slack message. Stress hormones like adrenaline and cortisol rise, and your ability to think calmly temporarily takes a back seat. This is often called an amygdala hijack.

Your prefrontal cortex, on the other hand, is what handles self-control, reasoning, and long-term decision-making. The problem is timing: it needs a little time to catch up. In the gap before it does, the amygdala is running the show.

So when you're angry, embarrassed, threatened, or overwhelmed at work, that first impulse isn't necessarily wrong — it's just early. It's rarely the version of you that should be making the decision.

Try this instead

  1. Breathe slowly. Give your body a chance to come down out of high alert before you do anything else.
  2. Move. Take a quick walk around the office, or even just stand up. A few minutes of physical distance from the trigger genuinely helps.
  3. Step away. If you can, leave the immediate situation before responding to it at all.
  4. Then ask yourself: "What outcome do I actually want here?"

Maybe the honest answer isn't "I quit." Maybe it's something closer to one of these:

  • "I need to understand what went wrong."
  • "I disagree, but I want to have this conversation constructively."
  • "I need some time before I respond."

The goal isn't to suppress the emotion

It's to create enough space between the emotion and the action for your thinking brain to actually join the conversation. The feeling is real and worth paying attention to — it's the immediate, unfiltered reaction to it that tends to cause the damage.

Sometimes the best career decision you can make is simply: don't send that message yet.
